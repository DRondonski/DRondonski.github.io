Three Lobed Rocket Nozzle Development

A documented progression from a failed first geometry to a working choked flow solution, followed by the next challenge: using more of the lobed exit effectively.

Overview

This is the most technically demanding project I have completed so far. I designed a complete three lobed rocket nozzle passage, prepared the geometry for computational fluid dynamics, built compressible OpenFOAM cases, and evaluated the results in ParaView. The first design did not choke. The second established a sonic throat and accelerated the resolved exit core above Mach 3.

Current status

Working CFD milestone: Test 2 reaches Mach 1 at the throat and approximately Mach 3.52 in the resolved exit core. The remaining problem is exit utilization. Much of the three lobed exit still carries slower flow, so the geometry now needs to be optimized for a broader high speed region.

Test 1: Failed Choking Attempt

The first case used the actual internal passage with all three lobes. It produced the correct qualitative relationship between pressure and speed, but the geometry did not sustain sonic flow at the throat.

The flow accelerated toward the constriction and static pressure dropped in the same region. Mach and pressure therefore moved in opposite directions as expected. However, Mach stayed below 1 and then decreased again. I treated the geometry as a failed model rather than presenting the visual trend as a successful nozzle result.

Test 2: Working Choked Solution

The second design uses hot LOX and RP1 combustion products in the complete three lobed passage. Flow travels from the circular chamber on the right toward the lobed exit on the left.

The animation scale includes an isolated peak near Mach 6.32 at an exit edge cut cell. I do not use that local artifact as a physical performance claim. The resolved centerline result of approximately Mach 3.52 is the meaningful exit value.

What Works Now

  • The throat chokes the flow. Mach crosses 1 near the intended minimum area.
  • The diverging passage accelerates the core. The resolved core continues beyond Mach 2 and reaches approximately Mach 3.52 at the exit.
  • Mach and pressure agree physically. Static pressure falls through the same regions where Mach rises.
  • The preliminary force direction is stable. Lateral force remains below 1 percent of axial force in the available result.

This is a working computational milestone, not a final performance prediction. The current inlet and exit flow imbalance is approximately 12.3 percent, so further convergence and verification are required before treating the thrust estimate as final.

Next Steps: Use the Full Exit

The throat problem is solved, but the exit is under utilized. The central region carries the highest speed and mass flux while substantial portions of the three lobed exit remain slower. My next objective is to spread the high speed flow across more of the available exit area while preserving the sonic throat and the Mach 3 plus core.

  1. Map exit utilization. Compare area weighted Mach, velocity, pressure, and mass flux across the full exit rather than relying only on a centerline value.
  2. Refine the transition into the lobes. Adjust the contour and area distribution so the expanding flow is guided into all three lobes instead of remaining concentrated near the center.
  3. Run controlled geometry variations. Change one feature at a time, including lobe width, transition length, wall curvature, and local area growth.
  4. Protect the working throat. Reject any candidate that loses Mach 1 at the throat, lowers the resolved exit core below Mach 3, or creates large flow separation.
  5. Verify the winning design. Continue to a tighter residual and mass balance, then perform mesh independence, viscous wall, heat transfer, cooling, and external plume studies.

The optimization target is not simply a higher local peak. It is a larger fraction of the exit operating at high speed, a more uniform mass flux distribution, and a credible improvement in usable thrust.
